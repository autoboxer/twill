# Studying effectively with Twill

Use this guide to choose how to study and turn trusted source material into
useful Twill content. Start with the difficulty you are having. Choose the
activities that address it. You do not need every card type for every concept.

For short instructions about current controls, open **Using Twill** in the app.
This document provides detailed examples and a process for preparing content.
The [research register](research.md) explains the evidence and its limitations.
The guidance draws on that evidence, but these particular Twill workflows have
not been tested in controlled studies. They do not guarantee retention.

To find a starting point, [choose by situation](#choose-by-the-difficulty-you-are-having).
For help judging answers, read about [fair grading](#make-fair-comparisons-and-honest-grades).
To create content, follow the [source preparation process](#turning-source-material-into-twill-content)
and [content brief](#content-brief-for-future-generation).
Check [current boundaries](#current-boundaries) before planning to use a feature.

## Start here

1. Decide what you want to be able to do without looking at the source.
2. Study an explanation and example so you have enough knowledge to try the
   question. You can make a brief first attempt before studying the explanation,
   but avoid spending a long time guessing without a useful starting point.
3. Attempt the question before revealing its answer.
4. Compare your response with the answer and its requirements. Review any
   important ideas or steps you missed.
5. Choose a grade based on your attempt before you saw the answer.
6. Return on later days. Practice a different example or comparison if you also
   need to apply what you have learned.

Recalling information from memory and reviewing it on later days provide the
foundation. Continue to read, study worked examples, solve substantial problems,
and check trusted references. Not all learning needs to become a flashcard.
[Dunlosky et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266),
[Yang et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000309).

## Choose by the difficulty you are having

| Your situation | Useful next action | Twill starting point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The subject is new or confusing. | Study a clear explanation and example. Review any background knowledge you need. | Read the Answer and feedback, then try a small Standard recall, Explain, or Problem task. |
| You recognize an answer but cannot recall it. | Hide the answer and try a specific question. | Use Standard recall or Type answer. |
| You need to recall a term in context. | Recall the term with help from its sentence. Check that it is not visible elsewhere. | Use Cloze. |
| You know the definition but not why it matters. | Explain how it works, what it changes, or how it relates to another idea. | Use Explain. |
| You confuse two similar things. | Identify the difference that matters, then apply it to another example. | Use Compare and contrast in Explain. More detailed comparison and choice tools are planned. |
| You can follow a solution but cannot solve independently. | Study the method, complete part of a problem, then solve another. | Use Problem. Guided sequences are planned. |
| You know one example but struggle when the situation changes. | Practice choosing and applying the principle in a different situation. | Create separate Problem or Explain concepts and link them with a clear learning objective. |
| You need to understand a diagram. | Identify its parts. Practice their functions or relationships separately if needed. | Use Image occlusion and Explain. Image labeling is planned. |
| You remember facts but lose the overall structure. | Recall the important sequence or relationships, then check for missing ideas. | Use an Explain prompt with key points. More response options for this activity are planned. |
| You repeat the same mistake. | Check your knowledge and reasoning, and make sure the question is clear and accurate. | Use feedback and Problem checkpoints. Flag unclear or incorrect cards with Needs improvement. |
| Reviews are taking too much time. | Remove unnecessary content and card types. Continue to review important cards that are due. | Set a session limit, find and edit cards, or archive material you no longer need. |

These starting points are alternatives. They are not a checklist to apply to
every piece of material.

## Work through your situation

### The subject is new

- Read a concise explanation and a useful example. Review unfamiliar background
  concepts before creating a large set of questions.
- Ask what the idea means, when it applies, and what a correct answer should
  demonstrate. Start with a question you have enough knowledge to attempt.
- Enable pretesting if you want to try a question before studying its explanation.
  Read the correction after your attempt. Select Skip if you have no useful
  starting point.
- Study an accurate provided example. You do not need to invent an example
  before you understand the idea.

Research on questions asked before instruction shows the strongest benefits for
the content of those questions, not for everything in the lesson. For some
concepts, studying supplied examples can improve learning and take less time
than creating your own examples.
[St. Hilaire et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-023-02353-8),
[Zamary & Rawson, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-016-9396-9).

### You recognize the answer but cannot recall it

- Use a question with a definite answer rather than “Do I know this?”
- Try to answer mentally, in text, or on paper before revealing the answer.
  Type when you need to practice spelling or notation, or want a written
  response to inspect. You do not need to type every answer.
- Use Type answer for short responses and add other valid answers in its setup.
  Twill highlights text differences to help you compare responses. It does not
  judge whether an explanation has the correct meaning.
- If you remembered only after looking, choose Forgot or Again. Read the
  correction and return later. Repeatedly reading the answer until it feels
  familiar does not show that you can recall it independently.

Recalling an answer mentally can help you learn. Speaking or writing an answer
has a modest additional benefit on average, but the evidence does not require
typing every response.
[Yu et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-025-10024-4).

### You need a term, expression, or detail in context

- Use Cloze when the surrounding sentence is useful context for the target.
- Hide text that matters to your goal, not a random word. Remove duplicate
  wording, visible definitions, or other clues that give away the answer.
- Use separate omission groups for text you need to recall independently. Hide
  several parts together when you need to practice their relationship as a whole.
- Add a broader Explain or application question only if understanding or use is
  also important. Filling a blank does not by itself demonstrate those abilities.
- For vocabulary, specify the intended meaning and direction. Recognizing a word
  and recalling a word from its meaning are different tasks. Practice both only
  if you need both abilities.

### You know the definition but cannot explain the idea

- Ask how something works, how ideas relate, what happens as a result, or why
  a decision is appropriate.
- Use Explain and add the key points that a correct explanation should include.
  Compare your reasoning with those points. Accept different wording that
  expresses the same meaning.
- Ask about a specific relationship or process. If you need an overview, specify
  which ideas it should cover rather than ask for everything about the topic.
- If you cannot explain the idea, review an explanation or worked example before
  trying again. Focus on understanding rather than writing a longer response.

Research supports explaining ideas in your own words, but the benefit depends
on the task and the help available. A longer response is not necessarily better.
[Bisra et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x).

### You confuse similar concepts or methods

- Compare the closest plausible alternatives, not an obvious right answer with
  unrelated wrong ones.
- Identify the deciding feature. Ask, “What makes this case A rather than B?”
- Check the reasoning, then try another case without the comparison visible.
- Use meaningful tags to organize related material. Optional mixed practice can
  vary due questions, but a shared tag alone does not make a good comparison.
- When choice activities are available, inspect why a tempting alternative is
  wrong. Selecting the correct answer does not necessarily show that you could
  recall it without seeing the choices.

Comparing related ideas and alternating between them can help you tell them
apart. The benefit depends on your prior knowledge and the material. Choose
related topics rather than mix unrelated topics just to create variety.
[Alfieri et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2013.775712),
[Brunmair & Richter, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209).

### You can follow a solution but cannot solve another problem

- Study a worked solution and identify why its important steps are valid.
- Complete a partially worked problem if a fully independent one is currently
  beyond reach. Return to the explanation when you need help.
- Then solve a related problem without its solution visible. Use the workpad or
  paper and compare both the reasoning and the result with the checkpoints.
- Leave a formula or reference visible if applying it is the intended skill.
  Practice recalling that formula separately only if recalling it also matters.
- Use a different situation when you want evidence of application. Changing
  numbers can support fluency, but does not necessarily test method selection.

For now, create separate Problem concepts with progressively less help. Sequences
that combine worked examples, partially completed problems, and independent
problems are planned. Removing help gradually can be useful, but the evidence
does not support doing so for every example.
[Atkinson et al., 2003](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.95.4.774),
[Zamary & Rawson, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9433-y).

### You remember one example but cannot apply the principle elsewhere

- Practice a different situation governed by the same principle.
- Include a similar situation where that principle does not apply,
  when a trustworthy contrasting example is available.
- Ask which method or principle applies and why. Leave its name out of the
  title and prompt when you need to practice choosing it.
- Create each case as a separate concept with its own expected answer and
  criteria. Open a concept in Library and select Link concept under Linked
  practice. Describe the learning objective and choose the related case.
  The connection appears in both concepts, but their cards retain separate
  grades and schedules. Select a linked title to revisit or edit that case.
- If useful, create an example that follows the principle or shows when it does
  not apply. Check it against clear requirements and an accurate reference
  example. An example that sounds plausible may still be incorrect.

Twill defers remaining cards from a concept and its directly linked cases after
you reveal an answer, an answer part, a hint or a reference. Cloze and Image
occlusion also defer related cards on presentation, because their visible context
can reveal other targets. Select the deferred count in Study to inspect those
cards or open them in Library. They stay due and earn no review credit.

The boundary lasts for the current session, including pause, navigation and Undo.
Hiding an answer or reversing a grade cannot remove information you have seen.
Starting a new session makes deferred cards available again. Linked practice
and mastery retries remain available as supported attempts. Relationships are
captured when the session starts. A shared tag or an indirect link does not
defer another case.

Card types within one concept share its content, so they do not provide separate
scenarios. Link separately authored cases when the connection supports a useful
learning objective. Tags can organize material without declaring that connection.
After revealing an answer in Study, select Linked practice and choose a related
case and a specific card. Attempt its prompt before revealing its answer. Choose
Recalled or Still missed to return, or select Return to session without recording
an outcome. Your original response and idea checks are restored. These extra
attempts are supported practice, not independent reviews. They do not change
review dates or count as completed reviews, even when the chosen card is due.
Tools for creating examples are planned.

Practice can help you apply learning to a new task, but success with familiar
cards does not guarantee that ability. Vary the question to practice something
you need to do, not just to make it different.
[Pan & Rickard, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151).

### You need to understand a diagram

- Use image occlusion to hide existing labels or relevant regions. Check that
  captions, legends, nearby text, and other labels do not give away the target.
- Practice names and locations separately from functions or relationships when both
  matter. “Identify this structure” and “What changes if it stops working?” are
  different questions.
- Practice with another accurate diagram when you need to recognize a structure
  in different images rather than remember its location in one image.
- Group masks when you need to recall their hidden content together. Check parts
  you consistently miss, even when you know most of the diagram.

The planned Image Labeling feature will let you add answers to an unlabeled
image. Image occlusion currently hides content already in an image. Selecting
from a word bank is different from naming a structure independently. There is
no evidence here that Twill’s planned labeling interface is better than occlusion.

### You know the pieces but lose the overall picture

- Hide the source and recall a specific process, argument, outline, or set
  of important relationships.
- Use prose, a list, or a sketch on paper. Judge the ideas and relationships,
  not artistic quality or resemblance to the source layout.
- Compare with a reference and identify the important omissions. Revisit those
  gaps rather than automatically creating a card for every missing word.
- Use a few structural prompts if a blank page gives you no useful starting
  point. When judging your response, consider how those prompts helped you.

Use an Explain question with key points for this activity now. More response
options are planned. Drawing a map from memory can be useful, but it has not
been shown to be consistently better than recalling ideas in prose. Adding a
mapping step can also take more time without improving later results.
[Blunt & Karpicke, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035934),
[O'Day & Karpicke, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000486).

### You keep making the same mistake

- Check whether you forgot an accurate answer, need background knowledge, used
  a principle incorrectly, or found an unclear or incorrect question.
- Use feedback to state the correct reasoning and why the tempting answer fails.
  Make the correction easy to find within the explanation.
- Flag unclear or incorrect content, or leave a note for editing. Do not memorize an
  answer you believe is wrong just to satisfy the card.
- Use an optional mastery retry after correction, then rely on later scheduled
  review. A successful retry helps you practice the correction, but does not
  show how much you will remember later.
- When appropriate, practice locating and correcting an explicitly labeled
  incorrect solution and compare your work with a verified correction. You can
  create this activity in a Problem or Explain prompt. Guided activities for
  finding and correcting errors are planned.
- Check feedback after uncertain correct answers as well as wrong ones. A lucky
  correct answer can still conceal a gap.

In controlled studies, feedback helped preserve correct answers that learners
were unsure about.
That does not establish that recording confidence on every review is beneficial.
[Butler, Karpicke & Roediger, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.34.4.918).

### Reviews are becoming a burden, or you have a deadline

- Remove duplicate questions and card types that do not serve your goal.
  Archive unwanted material. Keep useful material even when it feels easy today.
- Use focused sessions and manageable card limits, but return to unfiltered
  Study so preferred topics do not permanently displace other due material.
- Start with default scheduling unless you have a reason to change it. A higher
  retention target requires more reviews. It does not guarantee an exam score.
- For an exam or practical task, also attempt representative problems outside
  the familiar cards. Identify important objectives your library does not cover.
- Current sessions include only cards that are due. Studying cards early and
  planning sessions by available time are not yet supported. Keep your device
  clock correct and choose grades that reflect your attempts.

No cited research establishes a universal ideal card count or session duration.
The right amount depends on the goal, available time, and the difficulty of the
material. Repeated success across separated sessions is more informative than
a long streak of immediate repetitions.
[Rawson & Dunlosky, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023956).

## Make fair comparisons and honest grades

- Decide what a correct response should include before reviewing. An explanation
  can be correct without matching exact wording. A numerical answer may require
  units, a stated tolerance, or an assumption. Include those requirements in
  the question.
- In Simple mode, use Forgot for an unsuccessful attempt and Remembered for a
  successful one. In Advanced mode, choose Again if you could not answer correctly.
  Choose Hard for a correct answer that was difficult, Good for ordinary success,
  or Easy for unusually easy success. Use Hard only for correct answers.
- Judge the original attempt, not the answer you can reproduce after reveal.
  When the task requires reasoning, a correct result with faulty reasoning does
  not meet that requirement.
- Information included in the question is allowed context. An extra hint that
  supplies part of the answer is assistance. Judge what you could answer before
  you received that help.
- You choose the current grades yourself. Revealing a hint, reference or answer
  part before full comparison marks the review as assisted. There is no rule
  that maps each hint to a particular grade. One grade assesses the whole card.
- Correct an accidental grade with Undo last grade when it is available. Do not
  change grades to force a desired due date or claim that one answer proves
  every related card is known.

## Keep a concept useful without multiplying work

A concept contains shared content. Its cards let you practice that content in
different ways. Each additional card adds future reviews. A custom layout
changes presentation rather than the learning activity.

- Start with the smallest set of questions that covers your actual objectives.
- Decide which ability each question should test. A question can require several
  related steps when performing the whole process is the objective.
- Separate independently important facts or persistent trouble spots without
  splitting every phrase into an isolated fragment.
- Add other card types when they help you practice a different ability. Do not
  generate every supported form or reverse every question automatically.
- Use titles and organization that help you find material without supplying the
  answer during Study. Preview the complete visible question, not just its body.
- Use rich text selectively. Emphasize key answer terms, keep equations readable,
  and place an explanation beside the content it describes. Avoid highlighting
  so much that emphasis ceases to distinguish anything.
- Use templates for consistent presentation, not extra decorative cards.

## Turning source material into Twill content

Use this process whether you author the content yourself or ask an assistant to
prepare it. The goal is a trustworthy, manageable collection that trains the
abilities you care about. The number of cards is not a measure of coverage.

### 1. Establish the goal and inspect the source

Record the intended use, current knowledge, relevant source sections, and any
important deadline or references that will be allowed. Ask for missing choices
if they would substantially change the content. Otherwise, state reasonable
assumptions.

- Read the relevant material, including definitions, examples, diagrams,
  qualifications, and exceptions. Do not generate authoritative content from
  a title, search snippet, or unread attachment.
- Record the title, author or organization, edition, version, date, and a stable
  URL or local file reference where available. Add a chapter, section, page,
  figure, or another precise location.
  Distinguish printed page numbers from PDF positions when necessary.
- Separate explicit source claims from deductions and newly authored examples.
  Label and verify any additions. Do not attribute invented details to the
  source or create a citation that does not support the claim.
- Flag contradictions, unclear diagrams, missing context, or outdated claims.
  Do not turn unresolved material into a single confident answer. For changing
  material, or topics where errors could cause harm, verify claims against
  appropriate current primary sources.
- Paraphrase accurately and preserve conditions, units, exceptions, and uncertainty.
  Copy only what is necessary and permitted. Do not turn a copyrighted chapter
  into a substitute reproduction or redistribute images without appropriate rights.

### 2. Identify what you need to be able to do

Write specific objectives that describe what you need to identify, recall,
explain, distinguish, calculate, apply, or reconstruct. Identify the background
knowledge and relationships you need before writing individual cards.

- Prioritize central principles, useful vocabulary, meaningful distinctions,
  procedures, and errors likely to matter for the intended use.
- Include factual questions when you need to recall facts. You do not need an
  essay for each fact. Include applications when you also need to use the ideas.
- Do not automatically extract one card per sentence, paragraph, or heading.
- List each objective, the questions that address it, and their source locations.
  Record deliberate omissions so you can see which areas remain uncovered.

### 3. Choose the simplest suitable question and support

Choose the ability to practice before choosing a card type or layout. Decide
what stays visible and what must be recalled. Use the situation guide above.

- Supply enough context to answer without the original document open, unless
  using a reference is explicitly part of the task.
- Hide a formula only when recalling it is an objective. Do not name the required
  method when choosing it is the objective.
- Add a hint, worked example, comparison, or alternative case only for a clear
  reason. Use a sequence of activities when it helps, not for every fact.
- Keep explanations and feedback about common mistakes separate from the
  concise answer.
- For choice questions, check every alternative and explain plausible confusions.
  Avoid trick wording, obviously implausible alternatives, and unintended
  additional correct answers.
- For Cloze and image questions, check all visible clues and accessibility text.
  Keep the question understandable without revealing the hidden answer.

### 4. Specify how a response will be judged

- Give a concise expected answer and state what a correct response must include.
- Include other valid answers when appropriate. Distinguish differences in
  wording from errors that change the meaning or result.
- Check worked solutions independently. Validate calculations, units, assumptions,
  and whether another valid method would satisfy the question.
- For examples or explanations with several possible answers, provide clear
  requirements and an accurate example rather than a list of exact text matches.
- Decide which parts must be assessed independently. Check important missed parts
  even when the rest of the response is correct. Grade other questions separately.

### 5. Review the proposed collection before adding it

- Remove duplicates, unnecessary details, accidental clues, and unhelpful card types.
- Read each question without its answer. Is the task unambiguous and answerable?
- Check the answer against its actual source, not another generated card.
- Check that examples and counterexamples really illustrate the claimed rule.
- Show a representative sample when the material is large or ambiguous before
  generating a large collection. Explain the coverage and likely review burden
  without inventing precise time estimates.
- Label questions that depend on planned features. Use an honest current
  alternative or keep a draft for later. Do not describe unsupported controls
  or an importer as available features.

### 6. Refine after use

Look at later independent attempts and unfamiliar applications, not only whether
the draft reads well. Repeated errors may reveal a prerequisite gap, weak feedback,
an ambiguous question, or an unsuitable difficulty level. Change the relevant
content rather than automatically creating more cards or increasing target retention.

Treat personal results as feedback for improving your collection, not a controlled
experiment proving that a particular technique works for everyone.

## Content brief for future generation

For each proposed concept or distinct application, capture the following. This
is a drafting format, not Twill's database schema or an import specification.
Do not show all this metadata on the study surface.

| Field | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Learning objective | State what you should be able to do and why it matters. |
| Source | Identify the document and exact location. Label uncertain claims and additions. |
| Prerequisites | List the knowledge needed to attempt the question. |
| Title and organization | Choose a title, deck, and tags that help you find the content without revealing the answer. |
| Practice question | State the task, include the necessary context, and identify any allowed references. |
| Response format | Choose the simplest suitable card type. Mark any required capability that is still planned. |
| Expected answer | Give a concise answer and other valid responses. Include required units or tolerances. |
| Essential criteria | State what the response must include and which errors matter. |
| Explanation and common mistakes | Explain why the answer works and why tempting alternatives are wrong. |
| Optional support | Include a hint or worked example if needed. State which parts of the answer it reveals. |
| Related practice | Add a useful comparison, application, or activity for recalling the overall structure if needed. |
| Assessment boundary | Identify which parts should be judged together or separately. Describe help that would make the response no longer independent. |
| Verification | Record which sources, examples, and solutions have been checked. List unresolved questions. |

Use this checklist to review the proposed content.

- [ ] Content serves the learner's stated goal rather than exhaustively copying
  the source.
- [ ] Important claims can be traced to the source. Additions are labeled and checked.
- [ ] The prompt is answerable and does not reveal the answer through other
  visible content.
- [ ] The answer is accurate, includes relevant limitations, and can be judged
  using clear requirements.
- [ ] The response format matches the ability being practiced.
- [ ] Necessary context is present. Extra help is not mistaken for independent recall.
- [ ] Related tasks add value rather than duplicating cards with new wording.
- [ ] The smallest useful collection covers the important objectives and notes its gaps.
- [ ] Required Twill capabilities have been checked against the current app,
  not assumed.

When preparing content, inspect the supplied sources and check the current Twill
controls before choosing how to create the content. This brief is a drafting
format, not an import format. Do not invent a file format for importing it or
write directly into the database.

## Example of a small, coherent collection

This is an original arithmetic illustration, not an excerpt from supplied source
material. Suppose you need to interpret changes in rates expressed as percentages.

Begin with the distinction. A change from 10% to 15% is an increase of
**5 percentage points**, or **50% relative to the initial rate**:
`(15 - 10) / 10 = 0.5 = 50%`.

Choose from the following questions according to your needs.

| Purpose | Question | Answer criteria |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Apply the distinction. | A rate rises from 10% to 15%. State its increase in percentage points and relative percent. | State that the increase is 5 percentage points and 50% relative to the initial rate. Distinguish the units. |
| Explain the relationship. | Why do increases from 10% to 15% and from 50% to 55% have the same change in percentage points but different relative increases? | Explain that both increase by 5 percentage points. Dividing by their different starting rates gives relative increases of 50% and 10%. |
| Correct a misconception. | Someone calls a rise from 10% to 15% a 5% relative increase. What is wrong? | Explain that the statement confuses percentage points with relative percent. The relative increase is 50%. |
| Apply the idea to another situation. | A rate falls from 20% to 15%. Describe both changes. | State that the decrease is 5 percentage points and 25% relative to the original rate. Use the original 20% as the denominator. |

If you already understand the distinction, the application and transfer questions
may be enough. Add an explanation or correction question when it addresses a
difficulty you have identified. Leave the formula visible in a worked example
if useful. Hide it in an independent problem only if recalling the formula is
part of the task. If the starting rate can be zero, explain that this formula
cannot calculate relative change from a starting rate of zero.

Create these distinct prompts as separate Problem or Explain concepts. Link them
with an objective such as distinguishing a relative change from an absolute
change. Succeeding on one question is not an automatic pass on the others.

### Use a hint or reference when you need help

When creating or editing a concept, select **Add help** to add a Hint or
Reference. Use a hint for a useful cue or memory association. Use a reference
for a formula, source passage or supporting example. Both are optional and
shared by the concept’s card types. Put information that should always be
available in Prompt instead.

Try the question before opening help. You can reveal the full answer without
opening either field. Opening help before revealing the answer marks the review
as assisted, even if you hide it afterward. Grade the original attempt. Choose
Forgot or Again if help was needed to produce the answer. Opening help after
the final reveal is feedback, not assistance for that original attempt.

If you write a response, Twill keeps its state at the first help request for
comparison. You can continue writing, and that later response appears separately
after reveal. These responses last only while the app remains open. The saved
review records whether help was used, not the response text or a detailed log.
An assisted flag does not automatically change a grade or calculate a penalty.
The learner still chooses the grade for the original attempt. Help is withheld
during the initial pretest attempt, then becomes available during feedback.

### Reveal meaningful answer parts

Use answer parts when checking one part at a time helps you understand a larger
answer. For example, a solution can contain the chosen method, its calculation,
and its interpretation. Keep each part in the existing Answer document. Parts
can include text, equations, lists, code or images.

In the Answer editor, place the cursor in a block or select several blocks.
Select **Answer part** in the toolbar. To remove a part without deleting its
content, place the cursor inside it and select the same button. The controls
below Answer let you group a part with another part. Without authored groups,
all parts belong to one default reveal group.

On built-in study cards, **Reveal** shows one part. A group control names the
parts it reveals together. When there are no authored groups, **Reveal all
parts** shows every part. Individual reveal remains available. Hiding a group
hides only its members, regardless of which parts you revealed earlier. These
controls do not show other Answer content. You can reveal the full answer
directly. Template cards keep their authored layouts
and display parts as ordinary answer content instead of selective controls.
Put formulas or other context that should always be visible in Prompt.

Try the whole question before revealing parts. If you reveal a part early,
Twill marks the review as assisted and preserves your response from before
help. Grade that whole attempt. A part you missed still matters even if you
remembered the others, if the question required that part. If targets need
separate schedules, use separate cards
or concepts. Answer parts do not create schedules or inferred grades.

Parts and their groups remain available while you pause or visit another view.
Undo restores the previous attempt. These disclosure states and written
responses remain session only. First pretest attempts withhold selective reveal.

### Compare the important ideas

After revealing the full answer, Explain cards use their key points for optional
checks. Problem cards use their checkpoints. On other built-in cards, you can
check the answer parts themselves. You do not need to check both a rubric and
the parts that express it.

For mental recall, use Remembered, Partially remembered or Forgot. If you wrote
your response, use Present, Incomplete or Missing. Compare the response you made
before opening help. Later writing does not change that original attempt.
Leave a check at Not checked if you do not need it. These checks are optional,
and you can grade without completing them.

Compare meaning rather than exact wording. A different correct explanation or
valid solution method can satisfy the question. An answer part can contain extra
explanation rather than a requirement. Make the requirements clear in Prompt.
Use the Answer and feedback as a reference, including a complete example when
the quality of the whole response matters more than a list of separate ideas.
Accepted alternatives in Type answer are not separate required ideas.

Review incomplete ideas and any important omissions. Choose Forgot or Again
if a required idea was missing or incomplete. Hard means a correct answer that
was difficult to recall, not a partly correct answer. One grade assesses the
whole card. Twill does
not add up these checks, decide a grade, or schedule parts independently. If you
used help, compare the attempt you made before that help.

Checks stay with an attempt when you pause, visit another view or undo a grade.
They clear for a fresh attempt or mastery retry and are not saved after an app
restart. You can also use them while reading pretest feedback without creating
a review grade.

## Current boundaries

The examples use Standard recall, Type answer, Cloze, Image occlusion, Explain,
Problem, optional answer feedback, hints, references, answer parts, idea checks, pretesting, mixed
practice, and mastery retries. Authors can link independent cases with a learning
objective and revisit them from Library. Study offers explicitly chosen extra
linked practice without changing review dates. Answer-cued related cards are
deferred for the remainder of the current session. Focused review sessions include
only cards that are due. Choice authoring, guided sequences,
image labeling, reconstruction controls, tools for creating examples, and records
of learning across sessions are planned.
They are not available controls. References to them above describe future
possibilities.

Twill saves content, grades, settings, and saved drafts on your device.
Study responses, workpads, selected cards, and unfinished retries remain available
when you visit another screen, but not after you restart the app. Drafts are not
backups. Open Settings and select Backups to create a .twill backup or readable
ZIP export. Use a .twill backup to restore your library. ZIP exports cannot be
restored, and backups are not encrypted. Import and device synchronization
are planned.

Keep a separate copy of your source material. Twill does not require a hosted
service to evaluate responses, does not automatically judge their meaning, and
does not use audio. Its layouts adapt to narrow screens, but mobile packaging
is not complete. Update this document when related features change. Keep
instructions for available features distinct from descriptions of planned work.
